What you are seeing

The shaded half is the left half. The trace counts how many atoms are in it at each moment. There are no forces between the atoms apart from collisions, and no wall in the middle: any drift to the right is just what randomly moving atoms do.

The count falls from N to about N/2, then fluctuates around it. Those fluctuations are of order √N/2 atoms, so they matter a lot for small N and almost not at all for large N.

Try this

  1. Why does the count fall from N to about N/2? Is anything pushing atoms to the right?
  2. After it has settled, does the count ever return to N? Set N = 10 and watch for a while, then N = 60. What changes, and why?
  3. Compare the size of the fluctuations (the “Spread” statistic) with √N/2 for N = 20, 60 and 150.
  4. Click atoms to heat the gas. Does it change how fast the box fills? Does it change the average count once things have settled?
  5. A real gas has about 1023 atoms. What does that suggest about the chance that all of them end up on one side?
About the model

Hard disks (diameter 0.34 nm, mass 39.9 u, like argon atoms) in a closed 2D box, 28 diameters wide. Collisions between atoms, and with the walls, are perfectly elastic and are solved exactly (event-driven dynamics), so kinetic energy is conserved to machine precision between kicks. The simulation starts with every atom in the left half and a temperature of 300 K. The speed unit v0 = √(kBT/m) is about 250 m/s at 300 K. A kick adds the chosen extra speed along the atom’s current direction of motion. You can link to a setup, e.g. ?n=20&kick=6&speed=2.

Built as a teaching aid for statistical and physical chemistry.
