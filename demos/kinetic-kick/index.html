What you are seeing

Colour shows each atom's kinetic energy, from blue (slow) through yellow and orange to red (fast). The trace on the right is the temperature, which is just the average kinetic energy per atom.

Because the box is closed and every collision is elastic, nothing can add or remove energy except you. A kick adds speed to one atom, along its current direction of motion, so a fast atom gains more energy than a slow one.

Try this

  1. Click one atom. By how much does T change? Estimate it first: how much kinetic energy was added, divided by the number of atoms?
  2. Why is the temperature flat between kicks? Where did the kicked atom’s extra energy go? Watch its colour over the next few collisions.
  3. Lower N to 20 and repeat the same kick. Is the jump in T bigger or smaller? Why?
  4. Kick an atom that is already red, then a blue one. Does it matter which one you choose?
  5. Click several atoms in a row. Does the gas ever “remember” which atoms you kicked?
About the model

Hard disks (diameter 0.34 nm, mass 39.9 u, like argon atoms) in a closed 2D box, 28 diameters wide. Collisions between atoms, and with the walls, are perfectly elastic and are solved exactly (event-driven dynamics), so kinetic energy is conserved to machine precision between kicks. The simulation starts with every atom in the left half and a temperature of 300 K. The speed unit v0 = √(kBT/m) is about 250 m/s at 300 K. A kick adds the chosen extra speed along the atom’s current direction of motion. You can link to a setup, e.g. ?n=20&kick=6&speed=2.

Built as a teaching aid for statistical and physical chemistry.
