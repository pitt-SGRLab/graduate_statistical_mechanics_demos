What is liquid structure?

A liquid has no lattice, yet it is far from random: every molecule sits inside a shell of neighbours. The radial distribution function, g(r), is how we put numbers on that. Below, a real molecular dynamics simulation is running in your browser. Poke it, then build g(r) one step at a time.

The idea in three steps

  1. Count. Stand on one particle and count the others in thin rings around it, at distance r, r+dr. Do that repeatedly as the particles move.
  2. Compare. Bigger rings hold more particles even if nothing is arranged at all. An ideal gas of the same density would give ρ·2πr·dr neighbours in each ring. That is the "no structure" baseline.
  3. Divide and average. g(r) = ⟨actual count⟩ / (ideal-gas count), averaged over every particle and over time. g = 1 means no structure, g > 1 means neighbours are favoured at that distance, g < 1 means they are avoided.

This is a two-dimensional fluid, so rings are circles with area 2πrdr. In a real 3D liquid the shells have volume 4πr²dr; everything else is identical.

Try this

  1. Pick Gas. Why is g(r) nearly flat at 1 beyond a small bump? Why is g = 0 at small r?
  2. Pick Liquid. Where is the first peak, in units of σ? What does the second peak tell you about a neighbour's neighbour? Why does g return to 1 at large r?
  3. Hover over a peak and then a trough of g(r). Which particles in the box light up?
  4. Pick Solid. How many neighbours are in the first shell? Count the peaks and compare with the liquid.
  5. On the liquid, press Pin g(r) and slowly raise T. What happens to the peaks first?
  6. Switch to Repulsive only at the same density. The attraction is gone, but is the structure? What does that say about where liquid structure comes from?
About the model

320 particles in a periodic two-dimensional box, in reduced Lennard-Jones units (σ = ε = mass = kB = 1). Pair potential: u(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)12 − (σ/r)6], cut at 2.5σ; "Repulsive only" keeps just the repulsive core (Weeks–Chandler–Andersen). Integration is velocity Verlet with Δt = 0.005, and a weak-coupling (Berendsen) thermostat sets the temperature. For argon, σ ≈ 0.34 nm and ε/kB ≈ 120 K, so T = 0.55 would be roughly 66 K. The "pull" on the reference particle is a spring to your pointer.

Everything is computed in your browser; the page is plain HTML and JavaScript with no dependencies. You can link straight to a state, e.g. ?preset=liquid&view=g or ?preset=solid&T=0.4 (options: preset = gas | liquid | solid | repulsive, rho, T, model = lj | wca, view = count | ideal | g), which is handy for a QR code on a worksheet.
